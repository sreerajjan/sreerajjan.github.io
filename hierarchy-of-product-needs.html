The Hierarchy of Product Needs

Utility, usability, experience — a Maslow-inspired hierarchy for diagnosing product problems

Framework · Principles

Most product problems have a dominant failure layer. Getting the diagnosis right improves the odds of choosing the right intervention. Getting it wrong is the most common reason product teams solve the wrong problem with confidence.

The framework

The Hierarchy of Product Needs is a diagnostic model for separating three kinds of product failure: whether the product works, how much effort it takes to use, and how the interaction feels. The structure is borrowed from Maslow — three tiers, base to apex — but the framework is diagnostic, not motivational.

Within a single interaction, higher layers depend on the lower ones. Across a longer customer relationship, that dependency becomes softer.

This is a diagnostic hierarchy, not a delivery sequence. Teams can work on all three layers in parallel; the framework helps identify which layer is primarily failing so intervention is chosen against the right problem.

The concept

What each layer means

Utility — the base

Can the product deliver the intended job? Failures here are structural — a broken flow, an unreachable state, or a feature that promises X but delivers Y. When the search audit found that 100% of eligibility-related queries returned zero relevant results, that wasn't a usability problem. That was a utility failure. There is no point discussing whether something is "easy to use" when it doesn't work.

Usability — the middle

Given the feature works, how much friction does it take to complete? How many taps, how much thinking, how many wrong turns? Failures here are about cognitive load and interaction design — the task is completeable, but it is expensive to the user. Time-on-task rises. Drop-off climbs. Users start hunting for help. The feature functions; the effort to reach it doesn't match its value.

Experience — the top

Given it's easy, does it feel right? Does the interaction produce trust, delight, willingness to return? Failures here are emotional and relational. The user completed the task efficiently and never came back — or came back grudgingly, or completed the task and told a friend not to. The functional and the frictionless were delivered; the relationship wasn't.

Why the hierarchy matters — and where it isn't strict

The most common failure mode inside product teams is solving a top-layer problem when the base is broken. Redesigning the aesthetic of a page whose primary flow doesn't complete. Investing in NPS moves when the core task fails 40% of the time. Each of these looks like productive work. None of it fixes what's wrong.

The hierarchy tells you where to look and in what order. It is directional. It is not absolute.

A strong experience layer can create tolerance for usability quirks — the reason cult brands survive interaction gaps that would sink a challenger. But within a single interaction, the layer below has to hold. You cannot make someone feel good about a task that failed. You cannot make an unusable feature feel effortless through branding alone. The hierarchy holds within the moment. Across a relationship, higher layers can lend some patience to lower ones.

The practical use: when a metric moves in the wrong direction, ask which layer the movement lives in before deciding what to change. Falling retention is not a usability problem to be solved with a new onboarding flow; it might be, but it might just as easily be an experience-layer problem masquerading as one.

The metrics that live at each layer

The hierarchy is diagnostically useful because most product metrics are ambiguous in isolation. Drop-off, satisfaction, time-on-task — each one can indicate a problem at any of the three layers depending on what the user was trying to do. The framework anchors interpretation.

The measurement

These aren't exclusive metrics. The same metric can point to different layers depending on the underlying behaviour. The layer tells you how to interpret the metric, not which layer the metric belongs to.

Where trust fits

Trust doesn't sit on a single tier — it runs vertically through all three. In fintech especially, this matters.

Functional trust lives at Utility. Does the platform move money correctly? Are transactions secure? Are the numbers on screen the numbers that get deducted? A single failure here can sharply damage functional trust. Users don't rate financial platforms on charm when the transfer failed.

Interaction trust lives at Usability. Does the user understand what's happening and feel in control? Was the payment initiated. Did the repayment go through. Is this fee the fee I agreed to. A financial product can be technically correct and have strong brand sentiment yet still generate uncertainty inside a single interaction — and that uncertainty is where support calls, duplicate transactions, and quiet erosion of confidence come from.

Relational trust lives at Experience. Does this brand feel like it's on my side? Are the terms fair? Would I recommend this to a family member? Relational trust is slower to build and slower to lose, but it's what determines whether a customer stays through a bad quarter, a rate change, or a policy that didn't go their way.

All three matter, and none substitutes for the others. A platform with functional trust but no relational trust wins the transaction and loses the relationship. A platform with relational trust but no functional trust loses everything the first time something breaks. A platform with both but no interaction trust generates support load and duplicate actions one confused moment at a time.

The limit of the framework

The hierarchy is diagnostic, not prescriptive. It tells you which layer a problem lives in. It does not tell you which utility gap to prioritise, or which usability improvement will move the needle most, or which experience investment is worth the spend. Those are separate decisions, made with different tools.

It also assumes product purpose is stable. If the fundamental job the product is doing shifts — a lending app that starts adding wealth-management features, a payment app that moves into commerce — the layers themselves have to be re-anchored against the new job. The framework doesn't do that work for you.

Where it shows up in my case studies

The PDP disclosure experiment sat at the boundary between usability and experience. The page worked and customers understood the information, but the information density and disclosure sequence increased cognitive effort and made the decision feel heavier than it needed to. Reframing the sequence didn't change what was said — it changed how much work the user had to do to act on it.

The search audit was primarily a utility failure. In a 247-keyword audit, 100% of eligibility-related queries returned zero relevant results. No amount of usability polish could fix a search system that wasn't returning the information customers were looking for. The intervention had to start at the base.
