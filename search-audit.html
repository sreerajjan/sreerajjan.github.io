When search works for browsers but fails for owners

A 247-keyword audit of a major Indian NBFC's search surface

April 2026 · Mobile audit · Case study

Half of this platform's search volume comes from customers trying to check an order, download a statement, find a KYC form, verify their FASTag status. The search surface was designed only for the other half.

The finding that stopped me

For product discovery, 93% of queries return a relevant first result. For order and transaction servicing, 56% return nothing usable. For eligibility and credit score — a category small in count but existential in intent — 100% return zero relevant results.

The search bar looks like one thing. It's two, and one of them is broken.

Why this matters

Search is not a feature on this platform — it is the platform's first destination. Roughly half of all sessions reach the search bar within five seconds of login. For new-to-brand users, search is where they decide whether the app understands them. For existing customers, search is where they go when navigation has already failed them.

When search fails for a shopper, they browse. When search fails for an owner trying to download an NOC or check ECS status, they call the branch, or they leave.

The volume-weighted question I set out to answer: for every category of search intent, does the surface actually help?

The method

247 keywords, sampled from 1.87M real search sessions. Designed to represent the head, torso, and long tail — not just top queries.

Mobile viewport (375×812). All screenshots captured on real device dimensions, since >80% of traffic is mobile.

Playwright instrumentation. Automated capture so the audit was reproducible — same keywords, same viewport, same session state, same day. Not one-off manual screenshots.

Two-layer scoring. Layer 1 asked whether the right content appeared — Spot On, Partial, or Zero. Layer 2 asked whether users could understand, compare, and act on the results even when relevant.

Volume-weighted clustering. Each keyword tagged with intent cluster (product discovery, order servicing, loan servicing, account actions, eligibility, complaint) and weighted by search volume. This is what surfaced the browser vs. owner split — without weighting, the two groups look similar in count; weighted, one is served and the other isn't.

The choice worth defending: why score by intent match, not by clickthrough? Because clickthrough measures whether the user pressed something, not whether they got what they came for. Owners with no relevant result often click the least-wrong option and leave frustrated. The CTR looks fine; the outcome doesn't.

The headline: the surface is optimised for one half of its users

Intent cluster split, volume-weighted:

Intent cluster % of volume Spot On Partial Zero
Product discovery 39.5% 93% 4% 3%
Order & transaction servicing 11.6% 22% 22% 56%
Loan & EMI servicing 8.3% 59% 41% 0%
Account / wallet / card 7.5% 53% 27% 20%
Eligibility & credit score 2.1% 0% 0% 100%

Keystroke fragments (28.8% of volume) excluded from scoring — they represent partial typing states, not intent.

Two categories carry the failure.

Order & transaction servicing (11.6% of volume, 56% Zero). A user types my order and gets a page called All Stocks — the stock market discovery surface. There is no place in the app called My Orders; the query fails silently. Users who type cart get told to check their spelling.

Eligibility & credit score (2.1% of volume, 100% Zero). Small in volume, heavy in commercial intent. Every query returns nothing. Users looking for their credit score, checking whether they'll qualify, or trying to understand pre-approval get no result and no route forward.

The pattern is not random. The categories that work map to acquisition — someone shopping, someone browsing loans, someone considering a purchase. The categories that fail map to ownership — someone trying to service what they already have.

That's not a search problem. That's a product priority problem, made visible by search.

Three findings from the results-usability layer

Layer 2 examined the pages that did return results. Three findings worth walking through. The full set of seven is available on request.

1. Advertising occupies more real estate than results

A persistent banner appears on virtually every search results page: Payments Bank — Insure your main A/c from scams. On 375×812, this ad occupies 30–40% of the visible screen.

The problem isn't that the ad exists. The problem is that the ad-to-result ratio worsens as result count drops. On single-result pages — KYC, Statement, NOC, Offer, DND, Gold Loan — the ad is 2–3× the size of the actual result. On two-result pages, the ad roughly matches both cards combined.

The categories with the weakest returns are also the ones where the ad dominates the viewport. The user who typed NOC gets one card and a promotion twice its size.

The copy adds a second problem. The ad uses the word scams on a financial services platform. On a heatmap of first-glance attention, "scams" attached to a lifestyle image out-contrasts the text-heavy result cards — meaning an anxiety-triggering word catches the eye before the search result does.

When results are sparse, advertising becomes the visual answer to the user's query.

2. Loan results with no way to personalise or calculate

When a user searches for any loan product — Personal Loan, Home Loan, Loan — the results are 2–3 cards, each showing a different loan amount, tenure, and EMI.

  • Card 1: Insta PL — Upto ₹2.5L — EMI ₹8,666 — 36 mo
  • Card 2: Pre-approved PL — Upto ₹1L — EMI ₹3,255 — 39 mo
  • Card 3: Insta Salaried PL — Upto ₹6L — EMI ₹14,274 — 60 mo

There is no calculator. No amount input. No slider. The user is expected to pick a card without knowing which product applies to them, at what amount, at what tenure.

The user's actual question — mujhe X amount chahiye, kitna EMI padega? — is not answerable on this page. Instead, the page presents three pre-set product SKUs with fixed amounts that may or may not match the need.

The insight beneath the finding: the surface is built around product logic, not user decision logic. Products come with pre-set amounts because that's how the catalog is structured. But no user thinks in the catalog's shape. Users think in the shape of their need.

The fix is not a redesign of the results page. It is a reconception of what a loan search result is. The card shouldn't be a product — it should be an input surface for the user's intent, with the product derived from the input.

That is a much larger shift than a UI change. Which is what makes it worth surfacing.

3. Similar-sounding products create decision paralysis

Related to the finding above, but distinct: the products themselves have names that make them indistinguishable to users.

Search Cards shown User's question
Personal Loan Insta PL / Pre-approved PL / Insta Salaried PL What's the difference between these three?
Laptop Laptop on Easy EMI / Laptop on Easy EMI Loan These are the same words. Which do I click?
Insurance Car Insurance / Personal Accident / TATA AIG Car Are these all car insurance? Why is accident here?
FASTag FASTag Blacklist / [Wallet] FASTag Is my FASTag blocked or not?

The naming uses internal product taxonomy — Insta, Pre-approved, Salaried — that is meaningful to the underwriting team and meaningless to the end user. Worse, the labels contradict user intuition: Insta implies speed, but Pre-approved should logically be faster, since it's already approved.

When a user cannot differentiate between options, three things happen. They pick randomly (poor match). They pick the safest-sounding option (often not the optimal product). Or they abandon.

Each shows up in the funnel differently, and none of them look like a naming problem. But that's what it is.

What happened next

The audit landed with the SVP. Its immediate outcome was not a shipped fix on the results page — it was structural.

Search had, until this audit, been an unowned surface. It sat between the product teams (each of whom owned their own PDPs) and the platform team (who owned navigation). The results page was rendered by a set of merchandising rules that no PM had explicitly designed and no team was accountable for.

The audit's finding — that half of the search volume was structurally unserved — moved search from unowned surface to chartered product priority. That was the outcome I was after. Individual findings would have surfaced test opportunities; the systemic pattern surfaced the need for ownership.

Several findings have since been actioned. Several remain open. The larger question the audit raised — should this platform be built for browsers or owners? — is a strategy question, not a search question, and is still being worked through.

What I would do differently

One: the audit relied heavily on my judgment for the Layer 1 intent-match scores. I applied a rubric, but the rubric was mine. A second rater on a subsample would have made the scoring more defensible. In practice no one has challenged it, but the discipline matters.

Two: I under-invested in Layer 2 relative to Layer 1. The seven usability findings are strong, but I could have run them past 5–8 target users in short unmoderated tests before finalising the deck. The findings would have shifted from observed friction to validated friction — a different level of evidence for a stakeholder audience. Time constraint at the time; a real gap in the method.
